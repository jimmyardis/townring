The walled city still pays.

Three patches of one city, one tax structure, three patterns of development. The original 1704 walled city, the historic Cannonborough-Elliotborough neighborhood, and a same-size chunk of Savannah Highway in West Ashley. Per acre, the older patterns out-earn the modern suburban one by an order of magnitude.

The three areas

Three patches of Charleston, same governance, very different per-acre realities.

All three sit inside the City of Charleston. All three are governed under the combined 313.6-mill rate and the SC 4%/6% assessment ratios. The variable is the pattern of development, nothing else.

What's in the 11 acres of walled-city civic land

The architectural and civic fabric that made Charleston famous — and none of it pays property tax.

Five churches, four museums, two government buildings, two cemeteries, and a private school. The Old Exchange, the Powder Magazine, City Hall, St. Michael's, Circular Church, St. Philip's. Together 10.7 acres or 14% of the walled city. The remaining 43.3 taxable acres carry the full $24.6M annual tax bill — and still beat the suburban strip by 25× per acre.

Intersection density

A street network that diffuses traffic rather than concentrating it.

The economic argument has a structural twin: more intersections per acre means more route choices, slower average car speeds, more pedestrian-exposed value-creating frontage. Counted via OpenStreetMap, drivable named roads only (a conservative count — Charleston's alley network shows 0.60 intersections/acre in the walled city on the reconstructed 1844 map).

The fine-grained pre-zoning street network diffuses traffic across many paths. The suburban arterial pattern concentrates it on a few. Even with the conservative OSM count, the two historic patches have 1.6× and 1.8× the intersection density of the suburban one.

Use mix by acreage share

What each area's land is being used for.

Walled City and CENA are predominantly residential by acreage, with finely-grained commercial, institutional, and specialty uses woven throughout. The Savannah Highway strip has a higher commercial share (19%) but the parcels are larger and the building density is much lower.

Land-to-improvement value ratio

On the Savannah Highway strip, nearly half the value is in the dirt.

A district where most of the value sits in the land — not in what's been built on top of it — is structurally underdeveloped. Urban3 calls this the "Detroit problem" indicator: when the buildings stop carrying their weight, the city is structurally rolling over its capital. This is what happens when most of an acre is parking lot.

Methodology, part 1

Data sources and tax calculation.

Data sources

Parcel data: Charleston County CDC_ParcelMap ArcGIS REST service. Each parcel carries Appraised, Land Appr, Imp Appr, Class Code, Legal Residence flag, owner, sale price + date.
Boundaries: Derived from centroids of parcels addressed on each bounding street. CENA boundary approximated from CENA Two Boroughs Improvement Committee 2022 "Taking Stock" report.
Intersections: OpenStreetMap via Overpass API. Drivable named roads only.
Millage / ratios: Charleston County Auditor 2024 Millage Card (313.6 mills for TD 71/63/99) + SC Code Ann. § 12-43-220.

Tax calculation

Per parcel: Appraised × Ratio × 313.6 ÷ 1000 − LOST credit
Ratio: 4% if Legal Residence = Y; 6% otherwise.
LOST credit: 0.00090 × taxable amount.
Civic / exempt: Class codes 670–720 (government, schools, religious, museums) + 451 (ROW). Excluded from "taxable acre" calculations.
Land deduplication: Shapely unary_union of parcel geometries (corrects for Charleston condo parcels each carrying the full building footprint as their geometry).
Methodology, part 2

Caveats and companion materials.

What this analysis is not

The walled-city polygon follows modern street centerlines, so it's slightly larger than the historic 62-acre figure inscribed on the 1721 Herbert plan — the difference is three centuries of street widening.
CENA's southern boundary is the irregular Bee/Morris/Mary line; the rectangle here approximates it. The Septima Clark Parkway expressway ROW inflates CENA's "streets" share.
The Savannah Highway polygon is one representative chunk; swap in Mt. Pleasant, James Island, or a Walmart-anchored district and the order-of-magnitude conclusion holds.
Tax estimates assume the standard rates and ratios; historic-preservation easements would reduce the walled-city figure modestly. None of those adjustments change the order-of-magnitude conclusion.

The pre-zoning pattern, even after three centuries of erosion, still out-earns its modern suburban counterpart by 18× per polygon acre and 25× per taxable acre. The intersection density and land-to-improvement ratios point the same direction. No modern American zoning code permits the development pattern of the walled city — and the walled city pays the bills.
